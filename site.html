    This website is a modern, translated digital tribute to the classic Jouët Sheriff 600 & Calife homepage originally hosted at sheriff600.chez.com.

    The Jouët Sheriff 600 is a classic family pocket-cruiser constructed by Yachting France in the early 1970s. Known for its distinct modular fiberglass lines, safety, flotation capability (insubmersibilité), and ease of towing, it remains highly popular among sailing purists and DIY restorers (often called sheriffistes).

    The Yachting France Hulls

    Explore the two core variations of this historical classic: the Sheriff 600 and the Calife.

    The Coastal Pocket Cruiser

    The **Jouët Sheriff 600** was introduced in the late 1960s to fill the growing demand for small, easy-to-handle family cruising yachts. Built entirely in GRP (Glass Reinforced Polyester) by Yachting France, it was designed as a trailerable, budget-friendly coastal cruiser.

    Its innovative layout includes a single mold cabin sole, an insulated hull cavity filled with 1.36 m³ of polyurethane foam making it virtually unsinkable, and four berths. The mast is stepped on the deck house, allowing easy stepping and unstepping by a crew of two.

    Revue Bateaux Review (October 1969):
    "The Sheriff is an outstanding pocket cruiser that prioritizes security and interior volume for its size. It sails dry, behaves predictably in light winds, and its polyurethane flotation makes it extremely safe for coastal cruises."

    The Luxury Inboard Cruiser

    The **Jouët Calife** is the premium sibling of the Sheriff. Sharing the same hull lines and structural benefits (including the polyurethane foam insubmersibility), the Calife was built specifically for sailors seeking a higher level of cruising comfort and mechanical reliability.

    The key differentiator is the standard installation of a **Renault Couach RC6 inboard engine** (running on gasoline) with dynafill starter battery and a dedicated shaft line, instead of relying on an outboard motor. The interior features rich mahogany wood trim, dedicated luxury fittings, a built-in galley counter with sink, and a marine toilet.

    Revue Bateaux Review (July 1973):
    "Sailing the Calife IOR proves that you don't need a large yacht to enjoy the safety and comfort of a real inboard diesel engine. The interior layout is refined, utilizing high-quality joinery, making it a true miniature luxury cruiser."

    4. Steering & Rudder Assembly

    Figures 6, 7 & 8: Transom & Rudder Assembly (Gouvernail)

    The steering system consists of a transom-hung, kick-up rudder configured for easy shoal water navigation.

    • Rudder Blade Material (43)Varnished Solid Mahogany (safran acajou)
    • Pintles (44)Stainless steel pins mounted on the transom board (aiguillots)
    • Gudgeons (45)Stainless sockets mounted on the rudder box (fémelots)
    • Wear Plates (46)Brass or teflon spacers to minimize steering play
    • Tiller Hinges (47)Allowing the tiller to swing upward in the cockpit
    • Tiller Bar (48)Solid Ash wood (barre en frêne)

    Rudder Maintenance: Periodically check the wooden blade for varnishing condition. Mahogany absorbs water if the coating is damaged, which leads to swelling and cracks. Ensure the pivot bolt is not over-tightened, allowing the kick-up action to function if the rudder strikes an underwater obstacle.

    5. Step-by-Step Rigging Guide

    Figure 9: Mast Tabernacle Details (Pied de mât)
    Figure 10: Beaching Legs (Béquillage)
    Figure 11: Sprayhood Canvas frame (Capote)
    A. Stepping the Mast (Dressage du mât)

    Ensure you have checked that all rigging wires are clear and free from kinks before attempting to step the mast:

    1. Verify all mast fittings, sheave blocks, shrouds, turnbuckles, halyards, and shackle pins are tight. Apply grease to threaded screws.
    2. Attach the turnbuckles and lanyards (aiguillettes) to the cap shrouds, forestay, and lower shrouds. Extend them to their maximum length, keeping at least 5-6 thread turns engaged inside the turnbuckle body.
    3. Secure the lower shrouds (80) to the forward roof chainplates temporarily using the lanyards. Gather the remaining rigging wires and halyards along the mast track and secure them with a shock cord (sandow), leaving the forestay free on the front side of the mast.
    4. Position the mast base tabernacle between the two aluminum channel brackets on the coachroof. Insert the pivot bolt in the second hole from the aft end.
    5. With one person standing on the deck pushing the mast upward and forward, raise the mast to a vertical position. A second person on the bow must immediately secure the forestay pin to the bow chainplate.
    6. Attach the two cap shoulder turnbuckles to the aft deck chainplates. Once secured, the mast is self-supporting.
    7. Pass the safety bolt through the forward hole of the mast step channel, locking the mast in place. Tighten all lanyards (aiguillettes) on the lower shrouds.
    Terminology Note (Lanyards / Aiguillettes):
    Instead of expensive and heavy metal turnbuckles, the lower shrouds of the Sheriff utilize thin nylon lashings (lanyards) wrapped multiple times through the shroud eye and the chainplate. This system saves weight aloft and acts as a shock absorber. The lashing friction ensures that once hand-tensioned and knotted, it maintains the ideal tension for the mast.
    B. Shroud Tensioning (Tension des haubans)

    At the dock, the mast must stand perfectly straight without leaning port or starboard. Ensure that the shroud turnbuckles on both sides are tensioned symmetrically:

    • Forestay TensionTension the forestay first (use the 2nd or 3rd hole on the bow fitting chainplate). The forestay must be the tightest wire.
    • Cap Shrouds (Galhaubans)Tension the cap shrouds symmetrically until they are firm but not overly rigid.
    • Lower Shrouds (Bas-haubans)Hand-tension the lower shrouds using the nylon lanyards. They must be significantly less tight than the cap shrouds.
    • Mast RakeThe mast should have a slight aft rake (lean slightly backward). Verify this by checking the mast track line from the cockpit.

    6. Sail Controls & Reefing Procedures

    Figure 12: Slab Reefing Layout (Prise de ris)
    Figure 13: Spinnaker Rigging details (Spi)
    1. The Jib (Le Foc)

    The deck jib tracks have fixed fairleads. To achieve the correct sheeting angle and leach tension under different wind conditions, adjust the height of the tack point at the forestay. Use the tack chain link adjustment: if the top of the jib flutters (spills wind) too early, raise the tack point relative to the deck and note the link count for future use. Apply the same rule when tuning the Genoa.

    2. The Mainsail (La Grand'Voile)

    Adjust the draft depth using the outhaul and halyard tension:

    • Light Winds & DownwindKeep the outhaul along the boom slightly loose and ease the halyard tension slightly to allow more draft (fuller curve) in the sail.
    • Strong Winds & UpwindTension the outhaul strongly along the boom and pull the halyard tight to flatten the sail profile, reducing drag and heeling forces.
    3. Taking a Reef (La Prise de ris)

    The Sheriff's slab reefing system is designed for quick operation in growing winds:

    1. Bring the boat to a head-to-wind or heave-to position (sheet the jib flat to windward and put the tiller hard-a-lee).
    2. Ease the main halyard to lower the mainsail to the desired reef band.
    3. Pass the reefing line through the aft reef cringle on the sail leach if not already rigged, and pull it tight.
    4. Hook the forward reef cringle on the luff onto the reefing hook located at the boom gooseneck fitting.
    5. Tension the reefing line along the boom and secure it to the boom cleat.
    6. Re-tension the main halyard. Tie off the loose sail bundle using the reef points (garcettes) passing under the sail foot (never wrap around the boom).

    Renault Couach RC6 Inboard

    Official vintage owner's manual for the monocylinder inboard engine standard on the Calife model.

    Technical Characteristics

    The Renault Couach RC6 single-cylinder gasoline engine is specifically designed for the propulsion of small marine units:

    • Cylinder HeadDetachable cylinder head, easily removable for piston and valve inspection.
    • CrankshaftForged and balanced steel crankshaft with flywheel.
    • Connecting RodDrop-forged steel with thin-shell bearings.
    • PistonTin-plated aluminum piston with five rings (including two oil scrapers).
    • ValvesSide valves in high-strength stainless steel (easy disassembly for grinding on board).
    • Gearing/DistributionHelical-cut distribution gears, exceptionally silent.
    • Cooling SystemDirect water circulation with wax-bath thermostat control.
    • Fuel/CarburetorWatertight Solex carburetor, anti-roll type with flame arrester and drip tray.
    • Electrical EquipmentWatertight 12V Bosch Dynastart for starting and battery charging.

    Weight & Dimensions

    • Dry Weight (Complete Group)98 kg (216 lbs)
    • Overall Length575 mm
    • Overall Width468 mm
    • Overall Height506 mm
    • Max. Installation TiltUp to 15° (approx. 30% slope)
    • Fuel TypeRegular gasoline

    Tightening Torques

    • Cylinder Head Studs3 m-kg
    • Cylinder Head Bolts/Nuts7 m-kg
    • Connecting Rod Bolts4 m-kg
    • Flywheel Fixing NutTighten with a wrench and 1-meter extension lever

    Before Starting

    1. Ventilate the engine compartment by opening the cabin hatch to dissipate any potential gasoline fumes.
    2. S'assure that the throttle and clutch linkage controls move freely.
    3. Check the oil level: loosen the locking screw (A) on the manual sump drain pump, extract pump (B) from its housing, and ensure the oil level reaches the top mark on the dipstick (C) without exceeding it. Adding oil also establishes the oil level for the reversing gear line.
    4. Open the fuel valve and the seawater intake valve.

    Starting the Engine

    1. Switch on the ignition by pulling the key to the first notch (this runs the bilge ventilation blower).
    2. After approximately 30 seconds, pull the key fully to establish ignition contact (which stops the blower) and press the start button.
    3. In cold weather, close the choke valve (volet d'air) for a few engine revolutions, then open it gradually.
    4. Once the engine runs, immediately check that seawater is flowing out of the transom exhaust. Never run the water pump dry.

    IMPORTANT: Never rev the engine in neutral above 1,800 RPM. This can cause severe damage to the Dynastart.

    Periodic Maintenance

    Every 25 Hours: Check engine oil level. Lubricate linkage articulations. Clean the Solex carburetor's decantation filter bowl.
    Every 100 Hours: Drain engine oil (while warm) using the built-in manual hand pump. Replace spark plugs (Champion D 21). Clean the ignition breaker points and adjust the point gap to 0.4 mm. Spark plug electrode gap: 0.7 to 0.8 mm.

    Winterization (Hivernage)

    1. Close the fuel valve and run the engine at idle until it stalls due to fuel starvation (emptying the Solex carb bowl).
    2. Drain water from the cylinder block by opening the drain cock located on the block below the carburetor.
    3. Remove the seawater pump cover and dry the neoprene rotor to prevent it from sticking to the housing during winter.
    4. Remove the spark plug, pour one tablespoon of clean engine oil into the spark plug hole, turn the engine by hand a few revolutions to coat the cylinder walls, then re-fit the spark plug loosely.
    5. Remove the starting battery and store it in a dry, frost-free location.
